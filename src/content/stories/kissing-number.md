Imagine someone announcing that they have found room for one more guest at a crowded table. Before deciding whether to be impressed, you would want to know the rules. Did they bring another chair? Make everyone squeeze together? Or discover a genuinely better arrangement within exactly the same space?

That is a useful starting point for the kissing-number story. The objects cannot shrink. They cannot overlap. Each must touch the central object. The achievement has to come from their arrangement. The word “more” sounds simple; the constraints make it interesting.

## A small number with a large question behind it

On May 14, 2025, Google DeepMind announced that AlphaEvolve had found an eleven-dimensional configuration with **593 spheres touching a central sphere**, improving the previous lower bound of 592. That release is the earliest public announcement of this particular finding located for this story. The verified chronology begins with an announcement, not an earlier rumor. [Read DeepMind’s announcement.](https://deepmind.google/blog/alphaevolve-a-gemini-powered-coding-agent-for-designing-advanced-algorithms/)

A lower bound is a promise backed by an example: at least this many fit. It is not a ceiling. Finding room for 593 does not establish that 594 is impossible. Think of the difference between showing someone a working seating plan and proving that no better seating plan could ever exist. The first advances knowledge; the second asks for a different kind of argument.

Eleven dimensions make the example difficult to picture, but we can still understand its rules. In the accompanying plate, six circles surround one circle. That picture teaches contact and non-overlap. It does not show the research configuration, and there is no hidden seventh circle waiting to be squeezed into the drawing. The new possibility belongs to a different mathematical space.

## The story was already moving before AI made this headline

Months earlier, Gregory Barber’s January 2025 Quanta article had introduced general readers to Anqi Li and Henry Cohn’s work in dimensions 17 through 21. Their arrangements departed from familiar highly symmetric constructions. Mathematicians quoted in the article were interested in the unexpected structures, not simply the larger counts. This was a separate human research story, published before AlphaEvolve’s announcement. [Read Barber’s account.](https://www.quantamagazine.org/mathematicians-discover-new-way-for-spheres-to-kiss-20250115/)

It supplies an important lens for the later news. A beautiful, orderly arrangement can be useful without being the best possible one. Familiarity can help researchers reason, but it can also make an unexplored alternative harder to notice. The question “how many?” therefore sits beside another question: “what have our preferred ways of looking made difficult to find?”

This is why a tiny improvement can be suggestive without automatically being revolutionary. The size of the numerical gain and the value of the method are separate things to investigate. A new arrangement may matter because of what it reveals, what it enables next, or how it was found. None of those follows from the headline number alone.

## An outside reader asks what the excitement means

Ernest Davis, an NYU computer scientist, offered a more skeptical assessment in commentary dated June 2, 2025. He asked how significant AlphaEvolve’s mathematical improvements were and said that he wanted assessments from specialists. He also explicitly disclaimed expertise in the particular problems. His response is useful as an outside question about significance, not a specialist verdict that the finding does or does not matter. [Read section 2.3 of Davis’s commentary.](https://cs.nyu.edu/~davise/papers/AlphaEvolveNotes.pdf#page=4)

For a lay reader, this makes room for a productive middle position. We can recognize an improved construction while remaining curious about its importance. “The previous record was surpassed” is one claim. “This changes how the field works” is a larger interpretation. It deserves evidence about methods, reuse, and what researchers learn afterward.

The distinction also helps with public discussion of AI. An impressive search result can show a system’s capability without answering every question about mathematical understanding. Those questions need not cancel each other out. They tell us what to look for after the first burst of attention.

## A later headline brings the human work back into view

In October 2025, Aalto University published an account of Mikhail Ganzhinov’s research under a headline about besting AI. The details were more informative than a single winner-and-loser story: it described bounds of 510 in dimension 10, 592 in dimension 11, and 1,932 in dimension 14. It acknowledged AlphaEvolve’s advantage in dimension 11. Ganzhinov discussed searching highly symmetric arrangements; his adviser, Patric Östergård, emphasized the limits of AI. This was institutional reporting on Aalto’s own researcher, not a disinterested comparison. [Read the Aalto account.](https://www.aalto.fi/en/news/researcher-cracks-new-kissing-number-bounds-besting-ai-in-the-process)

The article’s publication date should not be mistaken for the date those results were first obtained. Nor does its headline mean that someone beat 593 in eleven dimensions. Its value here is to restore the neighboring work and the different questions being pursued. Research does not become one race simply because the coverage finds a race easier to narrate.

## When an apparent breakthrough was not yet a breakthrough

A later account by Federico Bianchi gives the search a human texture. Writing on April 12, 2026, he described an April 8 evening watching submissions on EinsteinArena, a separate agent platform he helped build. An apparent zero-overlap score looked like a new record. Stricter checking exposed numerical precision problems. The excitement had arrived before the evidence was ready. His post subsequently reports bounds of 594 and 604; those are the later project’s claims, not results independently certified by this story. [Read Bianchi’s account.](https://federicobianchi.io/research/2026/04/12/kissing-number/)

The episode makes a hard idea accessible: an overlap too small for a display to show is still an overlap. A near miss can be a valuable clue without satisfying the original rule. Checking is part of discovery because it determines which apparent possibilities survive.

It also keeps the original plate in perspective. The 592-to-593 change records a historical milestone, not a claim to display the latest record indefinitely. This narrative follows selected explanations and developments; it is not a continuously maintained table of bounds.

## What to carry back to the picture

Look again at the six circles. Nothing in that small picture can tell you how impressive an eleven-dimensional construction should be. What it gives you is the question, stripped of its intimidating setting: how can the arrangement change what is possible?

The surrounding story adds the rest. There are people trying unexpected structures, outsiders asking about significance, institutions framing achievements, and researchers discovering that a promising answer needs better checking. The extra sphere is the headline. Learning to ask what made it possible—and what has actually been established—is the deeper invitation.
